  \begin{abstract}
  Registered potential donors in stem cell transplantation may refuse to donate when matched with patients, causing supply shortages. To reduce such donor dropout, we developed behavioral interventions comprising messages added into the letters sent when donors are matched with patients in Japan, and conducted a field experiment to evaluate the effect of our intervention. The results demonstrate that the inclusion of our messages can reduce donor dropout and increase donor availability for transplantation. Specifically, the message emphasizing the limited number of compatible donors per patient increased the probability of donors completing the confirmatory typing stage by \(7.3\)\%. Since physicians can choose the best donor for proceeding to donation from those who complete confirmatory typing, this message increases donor availability for transplantation.

  \vspace{0.5em}

  \noindent
  \textit{Keywords}: prosocial behavior, field experiment, crowding-out, information provision, Japan Marrow Donor Program, stem cell transplantation

  \vspace{0.5em}

\hypertarget{intro}{%
\section{Introduction}\label{intro}}

Allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), a treatment with low relapse rates for leukemia and other blood cancers, faces supply shortages in many countries. Existing evidence---especially from organ transplantation---has focused on increasing the number of registered donors \citep[e.g.,][]{Kessler2012}. In allogeneic HSCT, however, there is an additional challenge: even registered donors may refuse to donate once they are matched with a patient \citep[e.g.,][]{Switzer2018, Haylock2024}. Mitigating such donor dropout is therefore crucial to stem cell supply. This study examines how behavioral interventions delivered upon matching can prevent donor dropout and alleviate supply shortages.

HSCT transplants healthy hematopoietic stem cells from a donor into a patient after killing the tumor cells by anticancer drugs or radiation. Thus, the HSCT requires compatibility of white blood cell types (HLA) between the donor and the patient.\footnote{When compatible unrelated donors are unavailable, several options exist: haploidentical stem cell transplantation and cord blood transplantation. The HLA requirements for these transplants are weaker than allogeneic HSCT. Notably, in Japan, allogeneic HSCT accounts for 20\% of all transplants \citep{JDCHCT2022}.} When patients cannot find a compatible donor among their relatives, who are more likely to be matched, they can search for unrelated donors through donor registries such as the National Marrow Donor Program (NMDP) in the United States, the Japan Marrow Donor Program (JMDP), and DKMS Germany.\footnote{While the probability of a complete match between two randomly selected individuals is less than 1\%, the probability of a full match between siblings is 30\%.}

However, donor registries worldwide face dropout among registered donors \citep{Switzer2004, Lown2014, Hamed2023}. Donor candidates matched with patients undergo confirmatory typing (CT) to assess their eligibility for transplantation. Because physicians select the most suitable donor from those who complete CT, dropout before CT reduces donor availability. Dropout before CT is substantial across registries. For example, 56\% of transplant coordinations in the JMDP do not reach the CT stage for donor-related reasons \citep{Hirakawa2018}.\footnote{As another example, in DKMS Germany, 23\% of donors do not reach the CT stage \citep{Haylock2024}.} Such dropout prolongs the time to transplantation and worsens patient outcomes \citep{Lown2013}.\footnote{In the JMDP, between 2004 and 2013, only 60\% of the patients registered with the JMDP end up receiving transplantation. Moreover, 58\% of patients who died (or had their transplantation interrupted because of poor health) passed away within 200 days \citep{Hirakawa2018}.} Thus, interventions to reduce pre-CT dropout are needed while preserving individual autonomy.\footnote{Many registries have already undertaken activities to recruit new donors, so the marginal benefit of expanding the donor pool is limited. Indeed, in the JMDP, most patients do eventually find donor candidates \citep{Takanashi2016}.}

In collaboration with the JMDP, we developed two information interventions embedded in the HLA match letter, which informs donors that they are matched with a specific patient and requests their participation in the transplantation coordination. Upon responding positively to this letter, matched donors proceed to the CT stage. The first intervention message (\emph{matching difficulty}) emphasizes the limited number of HLA-compatible donors per patient to reduce motivational crowding out among altruistic donors \citep{Bergstrom2009, Kurosawa2022}. The second message (\emph{early coordination}) highlights the importance of early coordination for patient outcomes to increase the perceived value of responding now and mitigate procrastination due to present bias \citep{Laibson1997, ODonoghue2001}.

To evaluate these interventions, we conducted a field experiment with the JMDP between September 2021 and February 2022. Our experimental participants comprised recipients of the 11,154 HLA match letters sent during the experimental period. To maintain randomness to the best of the JMDP office's abilities, we assigned treatment groups using weekly cluster randomization. After the experiment, we used the coordination data provided by the JMDP to estimate the effects of the interventions.

We found that our intervention increases the probability of donors reaching the CT stage. Specifically, the matching difficulty message increased the probability of reaching CT by \(7.3\)\%, whereas the early coordination message did not increase the probability of reaching CT. These findings are robust to several checks (e.g., adjusting multiple hypotheses testing across treatment groups) albeit without a prior pilot study or preregistration. Together, our field experiment suggests that the perceived scarcity of compatible donors reduces the motivational crowding out of altruistic donors.

The key distinction of our study compared with previous studies on information provision in donor engagement lies in the intervention objective and target population. Earlier studies mainly aimed to increase the number of registered donors and targeted the general population with lower baseline willingness to donate \citep{Li2016, Sallis2018, Robitaille2021, Goette2020, Goette2024}.\footnote{\citet{Heger2020} showed that establishing registries that collect potential donors beforehand is effective in increasing the supply of blood donation. For HSCT, such registries already exist.} By contrast, we aimed to prevent dropout among registered donors rather than increase the total supply. Our target population therefore consists of registered donors, who are more likely to have altruistic preferences \citep{Bergstrom2009, Ohtake2020}.\footnote{The effects of behavioral interventions such as message and information provision in health and medical policy contexts, including vaccination \citep[for example,][]{Dai2021, Milkman2021}, breast cancer screening \citep{Bertoni2020}, and cord blood transplantation \citep{Grieco2018}, have also been examined. Unlike our study, these studies targeted the general population, including people with low motivation to take action.}

However, some studies share the same intervention goal as ours. \citet{Switzer2018} examined information provision interventions at the time of matching but their study was not a randomized controlled trial. \citet{Haylock2024} examined the effects of commitment before matching, and thus the timing of their intervention differs from ours. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to document the effects of information provision at the time of matching among an altruistic population through a randomized controlled trial.

We also contribute to the literature on social norm messages, which tend to be effective when actions are publicly observable \citep{Bursztyn2017} and when the target population has low intrinsic motivation \citep{Shang2009, Kessler2017}. For HSCT, however, neither condition holds: donations are unobservable (to other donors and patients) and the target population is altruistic individuals already motivated to donate.\footnote{In particular, invoking social image can undermine the behavior of our target population. \citet{Allcott2011} showed that the effect of social norm messages is limited to households that already had low electricity consumption. \citet{Gneezy2012a} showed that in a Pay What You Want context, invoking social image crowds out the value of the self-image of being moral and altruistic and reduces willingness to pay for the good.} We instead show that a scarcity message (matching difficulty message) is effective in an altruistic population.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section \ref{experiment} provides an overview of the coordination process of the JMDP and describes our field experiment. Section \ref{result} presents the results of our experiment. Section \ref{discussion} discusses the welfare implications. Finally, Section \ref{conclusion} concludes.

\hypertarget{background}{%
\subsection{Background: Coordination Process of the JMDP}\label{background}}

\begin{figure}[t]
\includegraphics{image/flowchart} \caption{Flowchart of the JMDP Coordination Process and Sample Selection \newline \emph{Note}: Numbers based on our experimental data.}\label{fig:flow-chart-coordination}
\end{figure}

We first describe who can register as donors with the JMDP and how the donor pool is composed. Individuals eligible to register as donors with the JMDP must be 18--54 years and in good health.\footnote{Additionally, men must weigh at least 45 kg and women must weigh at least 40 kg. See \url{https://www.jmdp.or.jp/reg/requirement/} (Japanese; Accessed October 24, 2025).} Individuals who can actually donate must be 20--55 years. As of December 2024, approximately 560,000 donors were registered with the JMDP. Of these, 240,000 (42\%) were under 40 years and 280,000 (50\%) were male.\footnote{Data source: \url{https://www.bs.jrc.or.jp/bmdc/donorregistrant/m2_03_00_statistics.html} (Japanese; Accessed October 24, 2025).}

We next outline the coordination process leading to stem cell donation through the JMDP (Figure \ref{fig:flow-chart-coordination}). After a patient's physician registers the patient with the JMDP, the JMDP compiles a list of candidates based on the physician's criteria (primarily HLA compatibility). The physician selects up to 10 matched donors from this list. Subsequently, the JMDP office sends each matched donor an HLA match letter, recommending a response within 7 days.\footnote{Simultaneously, the JMDP office sends a short message to matched donors informing them that the JMDP has sent the HLA match letter.} Our experimental participants are all matched donors who received HLA match letters between September 2021 and February 2022. Upon responding, matched donors complete a medical questionnaire and indicate their willingness to donate.

A matched and willing donor undergoes CT within approximately one month to determine eligibility for transplantation. The transplantation coordinator asks matched donors and their families about their willingness to donate. Thereafter, the coordinating physician conducts medical examinations such as blood tests. CT can be skipped when a matched donor is eligible based on a previous CT result within a year.

The patient's physician selects the best donor from matched donors who have reached CT. Once the JMDP judges a matched donor to be eligible based on the CT results, the physician must classify the donor as the best donor, backup donor, or rejection. Only the best donor proceeds to final consent and donation, while up to four backup donors are designated and ranked by priority. If coordination with the best donor is interrupted, the highest-priority backup donor is promoted to the best donor.\footnote{Even after selecting the best donor, if the JMDP determines that additional matched donors are eligible, the physician must classify them as backup donors or rejections. However, if the best donor has not yet provided final consent, the physician can change the best donor to a later-arriving matched donor.}

The best donor must provide final consent after being informed of their selection by the coordinator and coordinating physician. Simultaneously, a representative of the donor's family must provide consent to the donation. After final consent is obtained, the donor cannot withdraw their decision to donate. They then undergo a surgical procedure to collect stem cells.\footnote{Collection methods (bone marrow (BM) or peripheral blood stem cell (PBSC) collection) are determined based on the preferences of the matched donor and coordinating physician.} The time from CT to collection is three to four months. Importantly, throughout the coordination process, a matched donor cannot access any information about the matched patient.

Our intervention involved adding two messages into the HLA match letter.\footnote{In designing our intervention messages, we were careful to avoid placing undue psychological pressure on potential donors. Specifically, we avoided using language that sounded like an appeal. Additionally, we only used information publicly available from the JMDP. Finally, the risks of transplantation were explained. The intervention message was approved by the institutional review boards of the Graduate School of Economics, Osaka University and the JMDP.} Table \ref{tab:list-message} lists the content of the standard letter and the two intervention messages. The JMDP also encloses a handbook that describes the coordination process as well as the medical questionnaire and donor consent form.\footnote{The handbook can be found at \url{https://www.jmdp.or.jp/pdf/en/DonorHandBook201708.pdf} (Accessed December 9, 2024).}

The matching difficulty message can reduce motivational crowding out among purely altruistic individuals by emphasizing the low number of matched donors per registered patient. Transplantation through the JMDP is a kind of public good because a transplant can be provided by other potential donors in the pool with the same HLA type \citep{Bergstrom2009}.\footnote{Up to 10 candidates can be simultaneously matched with a single patient.} Then, the presence of substitutable donors can crowd out the motivation of purely altruistic individuals who care about transplant outcomes, consistent with qualitative evidence from interviews with previously matched donors \citep{Kurosawa2022}.\footnote{\citet{Ohtake2020} estimated that 22\% of registrants had purely altruistic preferences, while the remaining donors had selfish or warm-glow preferences. Donors with warm-glow preferences, who derive utility from the act of giving, may choose to donate regardless of the presence of substitutable donors \citep{Andreoni1990}.} Thus, the matching difficulty message makes the donor's pivotal role more salient.\footnote{Two other potential mechanisms may operate. First, the message may reduce the decision-related uncertainty among matched donors \citep{Exley2016}. Second, the message may make the rarity of the opportunity to donate more salient, which may change donor behavior.}

The early coordination message emphasizes the value of not postponing the response to mitigate procrastination due to present bias. Donors with present bias may postpone their response to the HLA match letter because they incur immediate costs (e.g., consulting family members or colleagues) while they discount the future benefits of improved transplant outcomes \citep{Laibson1997, ODonoghue2001}. Highlighting the value of responding promptly may therefore reduce procrastination.\footnote{Commitment is a representative solution to procrastination. For example, informing the registry in advance about donation periods is one form of commitment \citep{Haylock2024}. The timing of our intervention differs from commitment-based interventions.}

Four experimental groups were established to estimate the effects of the two intervention messages. The first group received the standard HLA match letter with neither of the intervention messages, serving as the control group (StatusQuo group). The second and third groups received the standard HLA match letter including only the matching difficulty (MatchMessage group) and early coordination (CoordMessage group) messages, respectively. The last group received the standard HLA match letter with both intervention messages included to test the negative effect of information overload (BothMessage group).

We assigned the HLA match letters to the four experimental groups using weekly cluster randomization to maintain randomness to the best of the JMDP office's abilities (Table \ref{tab:assignment}). The participants were the recipients of the 11,154 letters sent out to matched donors between September 6, 2021 and February 27, 2022.\footnote{In a few cases, the same registrant matched with more than one patient and received letters multiple times.} Each week (starting from Monday) was assigned to one experimental group. The schedule was designed to be balanced across weeks and months.

Before conducting the experiment, we obtained approval from the institutional review boards of the Graduate School of Economics, Osaka University (approval number: R030305) and the JMDP (approval number: JMDP2021-04) for the experimental design and empirical strategy. We did not conduct pilot studies and did not register the experiment with a pre-analysis plan.

\hypertarget{data}{%
\subsection{Data and Empirical Strategy}\label{data}}

The coordination data provided by the JMDP included donor characteristics (sex, age, coordination experience, and residential prefecture) and information on the coordination process such as stages completed (response to the letter, CT, donor selection, final consent, and collection), days to respond to the HLA match letter, and willingness to donate.\footnote{The unit of observation was a coordination.} If the coordination process was interrupted before donation, the reasons were recorded as patient-side reasons, donor non-health reasons, and donor health reasons. Unfortunately, the data contained no patient information on characteristics or prognosis.

As additional data, we used the list of medical institutions on the JMDP website.\footnote{Data source: \url{https://www.jmdp.or.jp/hospitals/view2/} (Accessed August 4, 2022). This list includes complete addresses, the availability of bone marrow collection, and the availability of PBSC collection.} We aggregated this list at the prefecture level and calculated the number of hospitals per 10 square kilometers using the land area data from the Geospatial Information Authority of Japan. This aggregated dataset was merged with the coordination data using the prefecture as the merge key. We considered this variable as the traveling cost of coordination and donation.

The sample included \(11,049\) completed or interrupted coordinations involving matched donors in Japan.\footnote{One coordination involved a matched donor who lived abroad. There were 104 coordinations ongoing at the time of data collection, and their proportion was balanced across the experimental groups (F-test, p-value = \(0.383\)). We exclude these cases from the analysis.} Of these coordinations, \(4.5\)\% resulted in donation and \(63.4\)\% involved matched donors coordinating for the first time. The mean age of matched donors was \(37.9\) years (median 39) and \(62.4\)\% of coordinations involved male donors. The sample included slightly more young and male donors than the overall donor pool.\footnote{Altogether, \(17.1\)\% of coordinations involved matched donors living in Tokyo or Osaka, where hospital density is above the national average of \(0.21\) (\(1.86\) and \(1.36\), respectively).}

The primary outcome was whether matched donors underwent CT. Reaching the CT stage requires donors to respond to the HLA match letter, indicate their willingness to donate, and maintain their intentions. Additionally, late responses are associated with coordination interruptions before the CT stage (Figure \ref{fig:speed-CT-cond-response}). Further, physicians can select the most appropriate donor for transplantation from among the matched donors who have undergone CT. Thus, showing up for CT represents both a costly prosocial behavior and an important practical indicator of coordination.

  In the StatusQuo group, $22.25$\% reached CT. Breaking this down, $54.91$\% indicated willingness to donate, and $40.52$\% of willing donors reached CT (i.e., $59.48$\% of willing donors dropped out between response and CT). These figures highlight two obstacles to reaching CT: low willingness to donate at response and dropout between response and CT. Of these dropouts, $85.99$\% were for donor-related reasons: health conditions such as lower back pain ($75.24$\%) and non-health reasons such as scheduling conflicts and the lack of family consent ($10.75$\%).\footnote{\revise{\citet{Hirakawa2018} reports that, among donor-related dropouts, approximately 69\% are due to donor health conditions, 16\% to scheduling conflicts, and 10\% to the lack of family consent. In our classification, the latter two are grouped as non-health reasons.}} Health reasons are less likely to be directly affected by our intervention, whereas non-health reasons are more plausibly influenced by donors' coordination and decision-making and thus may be more responsive to our intervention. To examine whether our messages mitigate these obstacles, we construct two secondary outcomes: "positive intention at response" and "donor-related dropout for non-health reasons."

Panels A and B in Table \ref{tab:summary} present the intervention and sample size for each experimental group, respectively. Panel C presents a balance test of whether the randomization was successful. Although we find statistically significant differences in average age and in the number of holidays in the assignment week and following week across the groups, these differences are either negligible in terms of standardized mean difference (average age: the CoordMessage group is only approximately a year younger than the StatusQuo group; Table A1 in Supplementary Material A) or mechanically explained by the New Year's holiday falling in the following week for one MatchMessage cluster in December (Table \ref{tab:assignment}).

Although most covariates were balanced across the experimental groups, a few were unbalanced.\footnote{Age and the number of holidays did not affect CT, which is our primary outcome, in the StatusQuo group (Table A2 in Supplementary Material A). Therefore, the imbalance in some covariates is not a major concern.} In addition to a simple difference-in-means test, we therefore estimated the following linear regression model for HLA match letter \(i\) sent out in week \(w\) of month \(m\):

\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  Y_{iwm} =& \alpha + \beta_1 \cdot \text{MatchMessage}_{wm}
  + \beta_2 \cdot \text{CoordMessage}_{wm} + \beta_3 \cdot \text{BothMessage}_{wm} \\
  &+ X'_{iwm} \gamma + u_{iwm},
  \end{split}\label{eq:reg}
\end{equation}

\noindent
where \(X_{iwm}\) is a vector of observable characteristics, including donor age and the number of holidays. The parameters of interest are \((\beta_1, \beta_2, \beta_3)\).

There may be within-patient correlation because multiple donors can be matched with the same patient and physicians select one from among those who reach CT. Ideally, we would cluster standard errors at the patient level (or two-way by patient and the assigned week) but we do not have patient IDs. Instead, we cluster standard errors by the assigned week, the level of treatment assignment, which can also partially capture patient-level correlation when donors matched with the same patient receive letters in the same week.

\hypertarget{discussion}{%
\section{Policy and Welfare Implications of the Intervention}\label{discussion}}

To discuss the policy and welfare implications of the intervention, we examine the treatment effects on the post-CT stages: donor selection, final consent, and collection (donation). Because a physician selects the best donor from all who reach CT, welfare depends on both the number and the quality of donors in the pool. We argue that the intervention may have improved transplant quality despite the null quantity effects and that targeting clinically valuable donors could still improve welfare.

We first report the treatment effects on the post-CT outcomes. As a benchmark, when the JMDP sends HLA match letters to 10 donors and all reach CT, selection and donation rates can reach 10\%.\footnote{When no desirable donors are found through CT or when few matched donors complete this stage, a physician may be unable to find the best donor among matched donors. In this case, selection and donation rates fall below 10\%. Conversely, when the JMDP sends HLA match letters to fewer than 10 matched donors, selection and donation rates exceed 10\%.} In the StatusQuo group, the selection and donation rates are \(6.19\)\% and \(4.50\)\%, respectively; the gap reflects dropout due to patient deterioration or failure to obtain final consent. In terms of quantity, the effects of our intervention on the post-CT outcomes are neither statistically significant nor robust (Figure A5 and Table A4 in Supplementary Material A). The estimated effects on all three stages are smaller than on the CT stage: donor selection and donation in the MatchMessage group fell by \(0.71\) and \(0.48\) percentage points, respectively.\footnote{Logistic regression yielded similar results (Table A5 in Supplementary Material A).}

This need not imply, however, that patient welfare did not improve. Because more donors reached CT in the MatchMessage group, physicians could choose from a larger pool---potentially raising transplant quality even without increasing quantity.

This view is supported by the heterogeneous treatment effects by sex, age, and past coordination experience (Figure A6 in Supplementary Material A). The MatchMessage treatment encouraged young men with past coordination experience to reach CT (6 percentage points; \(p = 0.098\)) and increased their probability of being selected as the best donor (4 percentage points; \(p < 0.01\)). This result is desirable given evidence that young male donors improve patient prognosis \citep{Loren2006, Arai2016, Kollman2016, Shinohara2017}.\footnote{Using those in the StatusQuo group who reached the CT stage, we found that young men were more likely to be selected as the best donor, consistent with medical findings (Table A6 in Supplementary Material A). We also found that donor selection and donation were unrelated to the speed of reaching the CT stage (those who responded to the HLA match letter quickly or could skip CT).} The MatchMessage treatment may thus improve transplant quality by selectively attracting the most clinically valuable donors.

Simultaneously, the MatchMessage treatment promoted older men matched for the first time and women with past coordination experience to reach CT, but did not increase their probability of selection, suggesting welfare costs for these donors.\footnote{The MatchMessage treatment changed the behavior of both those likely to be selected as donors and those unlikely to be selected, resulting in average treatment effects on donor selection and donation that were close to zero. This reflects that, among those who reached CT, the MatchMessage treatment increased coordinations interrupted for donor health reasons (mainly disqualification based on the CT results) or for patient-side reasons (Table A7 in Supplementary Material A).} From the perspectives of welfare and cost-effectiveness, interventions targeting young men may therefore be more appropriate than a uniform message to all matched donors.

\hypertarget{conclusion}{%
\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusion}}

Our field experiment showed that the matching difficulty message increased the probability of reaching CT by \(1.63\) percentage points after adjusting for covariates and month fixed effects. Given the baseline CT completion rate of \(22.25\)\% in the StatusQuo group, this implies a \(7.3\% (= 1.63/22.25)\) increase. This result is robust to several other checks (adjusting for multiple hypotheses testing across treatment groups and using logistic regression) albeit without a prior pilot study or preregistration. Overall, the matching difficulty message expands the pool from which physicians can select the best donor.

To evaluate cost-effectiveness, we estimated that the message effect is equivalent to recruiting \(40,880\) registrants (see Supplementary Material B for details). Since \(100,000\) donors in their 50s will exit within the next five years because of the age limit for donation (55 years), the message effect can offset approximately \(40.9\)\% of the negative impact of this donor pool shrinkage.\footnote{Data source: \url{https://www.jmdp.or.jp/about/read/number/} (Accessed December 8, 2024).} Meanwhile, donor recruitment is costly. If the JMDP bears a similar cost to the NMDP (USD 150 per added registration), recruiting \(40,880\) registrants would cost approximately USD \(6.132\) million.\footnote{Data source: \url{https://fundraise.nmdp.org/index.cfm?fuseaction=cms.page\&id=1203\&eventID=675} (Accessed March 29, 2025).} Thus, adding the matching difficulty message could be a more cost-effective measure than recruiting new donors to address the shrinking donor pool.

The effectiveness of the matching difficulty message was attenuated when we simultaneously presented the early coordination message, possibly due to information overload. This suggests that effective behavioral information should be conveyed simply. Moreover, given that the matching difficulty message encouraged young men to reach CT and increased their likelihood of selection, targeted interventions directed at this subgroup may be more cost-effective than a uniform intervention.

Finally, our intervention may apply to contexts beyond stem cell transplantation. The matching difficulty message emphasizes the scarcity of potential cooperators in public-goods settings. In our context, it may have corrected donors' overestimation of available substitutes and reduced motivational crowding-out. Alternatively, it may have made the rarity of cooperative opportunities more salient. Although our data cannot identify the dominant mechanism, communicating scarcity is likely to be effective in other cooperative settings such as rare-blood donation.
